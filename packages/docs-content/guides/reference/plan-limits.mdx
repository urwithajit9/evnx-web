Until now the only way to find out an evnx limit was to hit one. The refusals are
clear — they name the limit and what to do about it — but a limit you discover at
the moment it blocks you is a limit nobody could plan around.

This page states them, and tells you how to see your own usage.

## The free plan

| | Limit | Counted as |
|---|---|---|
| **Vaults** | 3 | Vaults you **own** and have not deleted |
| **Versions** | 5 **per vault** | Every version stored for that vault |
| **API tokens** | 2 | Tokens that are neither revoked nor expired |
| **Activity history** | 7 days | How far back the audit view reaches |

Team and Enterprise plans remove the first three. Team keeps 90 days of activity
history; Enterprise keeps all of it.

<Callout type="info" title="A vault shared with you is not one of yours">
Vaults count against the person who **owns** them. If a colleague shares a vault
with you, it spends their allowance, not yours — and the versions pushed to it
count against them too, even when you are the one pushing.

That is deliberate: otherwise a free-tier developer could not contribute to a
team's vault, and a free-tier owner could raise their own limit by inviting
someone on a bigger plan.
</Callout>

## ⚠️ What "3 of 3" means

<Callout type="warning" title="At the limit, the next one is already refused">
evnx refuses when the count **reaches** the limit, not when it would exceed it.
So three of three vaults means creating a fourth is already rejected — not that
one slot remains.

The CLI and dashboard both mark that state as **full** rather than showing a
neutral `3 / 3`, for exactly this reason.
</Callout>

## Seeing your usage

```bash
evnx auth status
```

Prints your plan, vaults used, active tokens, and the per-vault version limit —
plus a warning naming any vault that is at or near its cap.

In the dashboard: **[app.evnx.dev](https://app.evnx.dev) → Settings → Plan and
usage**, and a line above the create form on the vaults page.

## What happens when you reach one

Each limit refuses the action that would cross it, and names the remedy. Nothing
is deleted and nothing is silently dropped.

| Limit reached | What is refused | What to do |
|---|---|---|
| Vaults | Creating another | Delete one you no longer need — the slot frees immediately |
| Versions | `evnx cloud push` to that vault | `evnx cloud history` lists them, `evnx cloud delete-version` removes one. The latest cannot be deleted |
| API tokens | Creating another | Revoke one you are no longer using |

<Callout type="warning" title="Why a full vault refuses the push instead of dropping the oldest version">
Most services prune the oldest entry when a history limit is reached, and a push
never fails. evnx refuses instead.

Pruning would delete your data as a side effect of a routine action — and for a
vault, the deleted thing is **a secret you may not have anywhere else**. A
refusal is recoverable; a silent delete is not.

The cost is real: on a limit of five, the sixth push fails until you remove
something. That is the trade, chosen deliberately.
</Callout>

## ⚠️ Activity history is a visibility limit, not deletion

<Callout type="info" title="Nothing is pruned">
"7 days of activity history" means the audit view shows you the last seven days.
It does **not** mean older events are deleted — `audit_events` is append-only at
the database level, so nothing prunes it and nothing can.

Upgrading a plan widens the window over history that was there all along.
</Callout>

## Self-hosting

Every limit is configuration, not code: `QUOTA_<PLAN>_<WHAT>`, where `PLAN` is
`FREE`, `TEAM` or `ENTERPRISE` and `WHAT` is `VAULTS`, `VERSIONS`, `TOKENS` or
`AUDIT_DAYS`. The word `unlimited` is spelled out.

```bash
QUOTA_FREE_VAULTS=10
QUOTA_FREE_VERSIONS=unlimited
```

An unparseable value fails startup rather than quietly becoming unlimited — a
mistyped limit that silently gave everything away would be a failure nobody
notices from the outside.
